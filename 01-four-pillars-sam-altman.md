## The framework: four things you must excel at

A startup needs a **great idea, a great product, a great team, and great execution** — and even then, luck.

> "The outcome is something like idea × product × execution × team × luck, where luck is a random number between zero and ten thousand."

Because it's a *product*, a zero in any factor sinks the whole thing. This lecture only goes deep on **Idea** and **Product**; Team and Execution are covered in Lecture 2. → see [[02-teams-and-execution]] (to be written).

**Caveat up front — don't start a startup just to start one.** There are easier ways to get rich, and every founder underestimates how hard/painful it is. *The specific passion should come first, the startup second.*

## 1. Idea

The "ideas don't matter, just execute and pivot" advice has "swung way out of whack."

> "Great execution is at least ten times as important and a hundred times harder than a great idea." — *but* "a bad idea is still bad." Most great companies start with a great idea, not a pivot.

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Idea = more than the product** | Includes market size + growth, growth strategy, and defensibility. Plans are worthless, but the *exercise of planning* is valuable. |
| **Think long-term** | It takes ~10 years. Long-term thinking is rare → a real edge. Build something **hard to replicate**. |
| **Mission-driven wins** | Easier to focus, you stay dedicated through the pain, and outsiders help more. "It's easier to found a hard startup than an easy startup." Derivative copycats don't compel anyone. |
| **Pick the idea you can't stop thinking about** | "Work on the one you think about most often when you're not trying to think about work." Wait for an idea you love. |
| **The best ideas look terrible at first** | 13th search engine; 10th social network limited to broke college kids; sleeping on strangers' couches. If they sounded good, too many people would already be doing them. |
| **Aim for a monopoly via a small market** | "Today, only this small subset uses my product, but I'm going to get all of them, and in the future almost everyone will." Dominate small, then expand. |
| **Conviction + ignore naysayers** | Fine line between right and crazy. If your idea's good, most people think it's bad — be happy, they won't compete. Good ideas don't sound worth stealing. |
| **Market must be big in 10 years** | Care about the market's *growth rate*, not today's size. Small-but-fast beats big-but-slow (desperate customers tolerate a rough product). You can change everything but the market. |
| **"Why now?"** (Sequoia's question) | Why is this the perfect moment — too early 2 years ago, too late 2 years out? |
| **Build what you yourself need** | If not, you're at a big disadvantage — get extremely close to customers. |
| **One-sentence test** | If it takes more than a sentence to explain, it's probably too complicated. Best ideas are very different from existing companies (Google, SpaceX); clones with a made-up differentiator fail. |

> Students' two biggest assets: better intuition for fast-growing markets, and the best environment they'll ever have to **meet potential cofounders**.

## 2. Product

Broad definition: support, the copy, everything in the customer's interaction with what you built.

> "Until you build a great product, nothing else matters." Founders spend their time on exactly two things: in front of the computer building, or talking to users. Almost nothing else.

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Build something users *love*** | A small number who **love** it beats a large number who merely **like** it. "Law of conservation of happiness" — total love is fixed in v1, so concentrate it. Love is far easier to expand than lukewarm. |
| **Word of mouth is the signal** | If it's working, people tell their friends → organic growth. Excuses about a big partnership "saving" you = real trouble. |
| **Competition rarely kills you** | "Very few startups die from competition. Most die because they themselves fail to make something users love." |
| **Start simple** | Early Facebook, Google (a box + two buttons), and the iPhone were radically simple. Forces you to do one thing extremely well. |
| **Be fanatical** | About details, copy, and support. (YC tell: founders who wire PagerDuty to their ticketing system so a 3am email gets a reply within the hour.) |
| **Recruit first users by hand** | Don't buy ads. Ben Silbermann recruited early Pinterest users in Palo Alto coffee shops. Do things that don't scale (read PG's essay). |
| **Tight feedback loop** | Turn user feedback → product decisions → back to users, repeat. "10% better every week compounds really quickly." Software's edge = loop measured in hours. |
| **No one between founder and user** | Do sales and support yourself early. |
| **Measure honestly** | "The company will build whatever the CEO decides to measure." Ignore total registrations; track growth, active users, retention, revenue, NPS. **Startups live on growth.** |

## Dustin Moskovitz — Why to start a startup

The 4 common (and flawed) reasons: **it's glamorous, you'll be the boss, you'll have flexibility, you'll make more money/impact.** Reality check on each:

- **Not glamorous** — heads-down hard work; real stress and founder depression. Ben Horowitz: "the number one role of a CEO is managing your own psychology."
- **You're not the boss** — "everyone else is your boss: employees, customers, partners, users, media." (Phil Libin) Your day is navigating conflicts and deciding who to disappoint least.
- **No flexibility** — "you'll be able to work any 24 hours a day you want." Always on call; you're the team's role model, so if you ease off, so do they.
- **Impact/money** — joining a high-growth late-stage company can pay *and* impact more (Google Maps, Gmail chat, the Like button were all built inside big companies, no startup needed).

> **The one good reason: "you can't not do it."** You're so passionate you'll do it anyway, *and* the world needs you specifically to do it. At Asana, the idea was "beating itself out of our chests." That's the feeling to look for.

## My action items

- [ ] **Idea:** One sentence. Why now? What makes it hard to copy? Will the *market* be big in 10 years?
- [ ] **Product:** Find a few users who would be genuinely bummed if it vanished — recruit them by hand.
- [ ] **Loop:** What's my single growth metric, and is it up every week?
- [ ] **Why:** Can I honestly say "I can't not do this," and that the world needs *me* to do it?
