## 3. Team

### Cofounders
Cofounder relationships are among the most important in the company. **The #1 cause of early startup death at YC is cofounder blowups.** Yet people choose cofounders more carelessly than they'd hire.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Never pick a random cofounder** | "Cofounder dating" / starting with someone you don't know = disaster. One YC batch: 9 of ~75 companies added a cofounder right before starting — *all 9 fell apart within a year.* |
| **Where to find them** | Best: college. Next: go work at an interesting company (Facebook/Google are "almost as cofounder-rich as Stanford"). |
| **Solo is bad, bad cofounder is worse** | Better no cofounder than a bad one — but the top 20 YC companies almost all have ≥2 founders; YC funds ~1 in 10 solo teams. |
| **What to look for** | "Relentlessly resourceful." The pop-culture model: **James Bond** — unflappable, tough, decisive, creative, ready for anything. Prioritize **tough + calm**, not just smart. |
| **Get a technical cofounder** | If you're not technical. "Software people should be starting software companies." The "we'll just be great managers" approach doesn't work well. |
| **Count** | 2–3 is ideal. 1 not great, 4 sometimes, 5 bad. |

### Hiring — first, try not to
- Few employees is a *good* thing. Lots of employees → high burn rate, complexity, slow decisions. Be proud of how much you do with how few.
- **Only hire when you desperately need to.** A bad early hire (first ~3) can kill the company outright.
- Airbnb spent **5 months** interviewing their first employee, hired only 2 in year one. They wrote down culture values first; Brian Chesky asked candidates if they'd take the job with one year left to live (later relaxed to ten).
- Once in hiring mode, it's your **#1 priority**. Recruiting the best can take a year — they have options, so you must convince them your mission matters most. "The best people know they should join a rocketship."
- **Time on hiring: 0% or 25%.** Nothing, or your single biggest block of time.

### Never compromise on quality
> "Mediocre engineers do not build great companies." A single mediocre hire in the first five can kill a startup (a big company absorbs it; a startup can't).

Bar to clear: **"Will I bet the future of this company on this single hire?"**

### Sources & evaluation
- **Best source = people you and your employees already know.** Personal referrals build most great companies' first 100 hires. Mine your network; have employees do the same. Look outside the Valley too.
- **Experience** matters for senior/org-leading roles, much less for early hires — go for **aptitude + belief**. Many of Sam's best hires had never done the thing before.
- **Three questions:** Are they smart? Do they get things done? Do I want to spend a lot of time around them? Yes to all three → almost never regretted.
- **Work on a project together (a day or two) > an interview.** If interviewing: dig into past projects, not brainteasers. **Call references and press hard** — "top 5% you've worked with? Would you hire them again? Why aren't you?"
- Good **communication skills** correlate with hires that work out. Want **risk tolerance + maniacal determination** (someone choosing between McKinsey and your startup probably won't work out).
- PG's **animal test** (describe each person as "an animal at what they do"). Zuckerberg: hire people you'd be comfortable (a) hanging with socially and (b) reporting to if roles reversed.

### Equity
- Aim to give ~**10% to the first 10 employees**, 4-year vesting.
- Founders are too **stingy with employees** and too **generous with investors** — backwards. Employees build the company over years; investors mostly write the check. The most generous-to-employees YC companies are generally the most successful.

### Retention & management
- First-time CEOs are usually bad managers — assume it and overcompensate.
- Don't tell your team they're screwing up every day (they'll all leave). **Praise the team, take credit for nothing; take responsibility for the bad stuff.**
- Don't micromanage; hand out real areas of responsibility. Dan Pink's motivators: **autonomy, mastery, purpose.** Do 1:1s, give clear feedback.

### Firing — fast
- Everyone waits too long. **Fire fast when it's not working** — better for the company *and* the person.
- Also fire people who create **office politics** or are **persistently negative** — toxic.
- The signal isn't one or two screw-ups (everyone has those); it's someone getting **every decision consistently wrong** over weeks. By then it's obvious.

### Cofounder mechanics
- **Set the equity split early** (first weeks), and make it **near-equal**. Unwillingness to give a near-equal share = reconsider the cofounder.
- **Vesting is mandatory** (4 years, 1-year cliff). It pre-negotiates what happens if someone leaves; without it, a departing cofounder leaves dead weight on the cap table and you can't raise. YC won't fund without it.
- **Same location.** Remote cofounding in the early days is very hard — communication and speed outweigh everything.

## 4. Execution

Most critical, least fun, **can't be outsourced.** Being a cofounder means signing up for a years-long grind on execution.

> "Whatever the founders do becomes the culture." Want hard work, attention to detail, frugality? You have to *be* the maniacal execution machine — you can't hire a COO to do it while you hit conferences.

**The CEO's 5 early jobs:** set the vision, raise money, evangelize the mission, hire & manage the team, and — the one founders forget — **set the execution bar.** Only the CEO can do that last one.

Execution = (1) figure out what to do, (2) get it done. Getting it done = **focus + intensity.**

### Focus
- Each day, 100 important things compete for attention. Identify the **2–3 that matter**; ignore/delegate/defer the rest.
- The trick to great execution is **saying no** — ~97 times out of 100.
- **"You get no credit for trying."** Points come only from making something the market wants. Hard work on the wrong things still fails.
- Set a few **overarching company goals everyone knows** (ship date, growth rate, key hires). Repeat them far more than feels necessary — walls, 1:1s, all-hands.
- Focus requires **good communication** — even at 4–5 people, a small breakdown means people work on slightly different things.
- **Never lose focus on growth and momentum.** Weekly metrics review. Be very suspicious of "we're not focused on growth right now because [rebrand / other thing]." Don't get high on your own PR.

### Intensity
- Startups only work at an intense level: **extreme focus + extreme dedication.** You can have a startup and *one* other thing (e.g. family) — not many. Be willing to **outwork competitors**.
- A little extra work on the right thing is decisive: viral coefficient **0.99 → flatlines and dies; 1.01 → exponential growth forever.**
- **Move fast AND obsess over quality** (Apple, Google, Facebook do both). "You don't buy people shitty computers if you don't want them to write shitty code." A quality bar runs through *everything*, not just the product.
- **Be decisive** — indecisiveness is a startup killer. Bias toward action. Mediocre founders talk grand plans but never decide.
- **Get things done quickly, in incremental pieces.** "Every time we talk to a team they've gotten new things done" is YC's single best predictor of success. Break even big things into small chunks.
- **Speed as a premium:** best founders answer email fastest, decide fastest, and **get on planes in marginal situations** — literally. (Sam's story: about to lose a first big customer, flew overnight, sat in their office at 6am until they met, they tore up the competitor's contract.)

### Momentum & growth — the lifeblood
> If Sam could tell founders only one thing about running a company: **always keep momentum.** Top-3 secret of executing well.

![Momentum: a viral coefficient just above or below 1.0 decides everything](assets/momentum.svg)

- A winning team keeps winning; a team that hasn't won in a while gets demoralized and keeps losing. **Never take your foot off the gas.**
- Software → keep growing. Hardware → don't let ship dates slip. (Getting the *product* right early is the best defense against later momentum loss.)
- **If momentum sags:** don't give vision speeches (save those for when you're winning) — get **small wins.** "Sales fix everything in a startup." When the team disagrees on what to do, **ask your users** and do what they say. Name it out loud: "we don't hate each other, we just need to get back on track."
- Facebook 2008: growth slowed → Zuckerberg created a **growth group** doing many tiny things; it bent the curve back up and became the most prestigious team there.
- Build an **operating rhythm** early: ship/launch on a regular cadence, review metrics weekly with the whole company. Boards add little to strategy but are a great **forcing function** on metrics and milestones.
- **Competitors:** the biggest false momentum-killer. "Don't worry about a competitor until they're actually beating you with a real, shipped product. Press releases are easier to write than code." Henry Ford: *"The competitor to be feared is one who never bothers about you at all, but goes on making his own business better all the time."*

## My action items

- [ ] **Cofounder:** Do I actually know them well (years)? Are they tough + calm? Vesting + near-equal split signed in the first weeks?
- [ ] **Hiring:** Stay tiny as long as possible. For each early hire: smart? gets things done? want them around? Would I bet the company on them?
- [ ] **Equity:** Am I being generous with employees (≈10% to first 10) and tough with investors?
- [ ] **Focus:** What are my 2–3 things today? What am I saying no to?
- [ ] **Momentum:** What's my weekly metric, and did it grow this week? What's the next small win?
