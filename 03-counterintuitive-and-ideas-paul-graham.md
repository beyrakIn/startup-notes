The frame: **startups are counterintuitive, like skiing.** When you want to slow down on skis, your instinct is to lean back — and you fly out of control. Startups have a similar list of instincts you must suppress. PG: "We used to joke our function was to tell founders things they would ignore" — and a year later they'd say *"I wish we'd listened."*

---

## The six counterintuitive points

### 1. Trust your instincts about people — not about startups
Your life so far isn't like running a startup, so don't trust your startup instincts. But your **interactions with people** are exactly like the business world, so **do** trust those.

- Founders' classic mistake: meeting someone impressive they have misgivings about, ignoring the misgivings, and later saying *"I knew there was something wrong about that guy."*
- Engineers wrongly assume "business is distasteful," so they tolerate smart-but-distasteful people. Don't. **Pick people the way you'd pick friends** — like and respect them, and know them long enough to be sure. ("There are a lot of people who are really good at seeming likable for a while. Wait till your interests are opposed.")

### 2. You don't need expertise in startups — you need expertise in your *users*
> "What you need to know to succeed in a startup is not expertise in startups. What you need is expertise in your own users."

- Zuckerberg succeeded **despite** being a startup noob (Facebook was first incorporated as a Florida LLC). He understood his users.
- Learning startup *mechanics* is unnecessary and maybe **dangerous** → leads to **"Playing House":** going through the motions — plausible idea, raise funding at a nice valuation, rent a SoMa office, hire your friends — while neglecting the one essential thing: **make something people want.**
- Why it happens: going through the motions is what school trained you to do for 20 years (extracurriculars ✓, gaming exams ✓).

### 3. Starting a startup is where gaming the system stops working
> "There is no boss to trick. How can you trick people when there's nobody to trick? There are only users... they're like sharks — too stupid to fool. It's meat or no meat."

- Faking works *somewhat* on investors for a round or two — but you're running a confidence trick **on yourself**, wasting your own time on a doomed company.
- **Stop looking for the trick.** Tricks exist but are an order of magnitude less important than solving the real problem. "Someone who knows zero about fundraising but has made something users really love will have an easier time raising money than someone who knows every trick in the book but has a flat usage graph."
- **"Growth hacks" = bullshit.** The actual growth hack: *make something users love, then tell them about it.*

### 4. Startups are all-consuming
They take over your life for years — maybe a decade, maybe your whole working life. Real opportunity cost.

- Larry Page "started running as fast as he could at 25 and hasn't stopped to catch his breath since." The difficulty is hidden — successful founders are like Olympic sprinters who never let you see them out of breath.
- **"It never gets any easier."** Problems get more glamorous (London office construction vs. a broken AC), but the total volume of worry never decreases.
- It's like having kids — a button that changes your life irrevocably.
- **Therefore: don't start a startup in college. 20 is not the optimal time.** Your early 20s are uniquely good for things you can't do later — plunging into whim projects with no payoff, traveling cheaply with no deadline, serendipity. There's **no trade-off**: you're *more* likely to succeed if you wait. (Zuckerberg will never get to backpack around Thailand; "Facebook is running him as much as he's running Facebook.")

### 5. You can't tell whether you'd be good at it
Your past gives you a read on becoming a mathematician or athlete — but nothing like starting a startup. A startup **changes you**, so you're estimating not what you are but **what you could become** — and nobody can predict that (PG spent 9 years trying; the honest word was "guess").

- Smarts are easy to judge in 10 minutes; toughness and ambition aren't. Swaggering recruits are no likelier to be tough than quiet ones.
- If you're **terrified**, don't (unless you get off on doing things you fear). If you're **merely unsure**, the only way to find out is to try — *just not now.*

### 6. The way to get startup ideas is NOT to try to think of startup ideas
> Make a conscious effort and "you will think of ideas that are not only bad but bad and plausible-sounding" — so you and everyone else get fooled, and you waste time.

Instead, **turn your mind into the type that has startup ideas unconsciously** — so unconsciously you don't even realize at first they're startups. Yahoo, Google, Facebook, Apple **all started as side projects.** The best ideas almost always start as side projects because they're such outliers your conscious mind would reject them as companies.

## How to have ideas: turn your mind into the right kind

The recipe (these three also get you **cofounders at the same time as the idea**):

1. **Learn about a lot of things that matter.**
2. **Work on problems that interest you.**
3. **With people you like and/or respect.**

![Live at the leading edge of technology](assets/leading-edge.svg)

- You don't have to work on technology per se — work on things that **stretch you**. (Airbnb's founders went to art school; they were experts in *design* and in organizing people to get projects done.)
- **Get to the leading edge of some technology — "live in the future"** (Paul Buchheit). Then ideas that seem prescient to others will seem **obvious** to you; you'll know they're things that *ought to exist*. (A grad student wrote VoIP software just to talk to his girlfriend in Taiwan without paying long distance — obvious to him because he was an expert on networks. Never made it a startup; that's how the best ones tend to begin.)
- The optimal college move isn't a vocational entrepreneurship track — it's **classic education for its own sake.** What matters is **domain expertise** born of genuine curiosity (Larry Page → search). Introduce the ulterior motive (the startup) at the *end* of the process.

> **Ultimate advice, two words: "Just learn."**

## Sharp Q&A nuggets

- **Non-technical founder?** In a domain startup (e.g. Uber + limo expert) they do most of the work. In a pure-tech startup, they "do sales and bring coffee and cheeseburgers to the programmer."
- **Business school?** Basically no — it teaches *management*, a problem you only have once you're successful. Go to **design school** if anything, or just do it.
- **First hires** should be **like founders** — self-motivated peers you don't have to manage.
- **Bubble?** High valuations ≠ a bubble. A bubble is knowingly paying high prices hoping to unload on a "greater fool" (late-'90s VCs). That wasn't happening — but "assume the worst" about your next raise.
- **Female founders** empirically have a harder time raising — solution is the same as for everyone: **do really well.** "Growth graphs have no gender — let them fall in love with that first."
- **When does a side project become a startup?** "When it takes over an alarmingly large percentage of your life."
- **Hire people you like → monoculture risk?** The advantage of hiring people you know and like far outweighs it. Empirically, successful founders just hire their pals.

## My action items

- [ ] **People:** Do I actually like and respect my cofounders — and have I known them long enough to be sure? Any misgivings I'm ignoring?
- [ ] **Users not mechanics:** Am I building expertise in my *users*, or just "playing house" (fundraising, office, hires) before I have something people want?
- [ ] **No tricks:** Is my energy on making something users love — or on growth hacks and investor tricks?
- [ ] **Ideas:** What leading edge am I living on? What "ought to exist" that seems obvious to me but not to others?
